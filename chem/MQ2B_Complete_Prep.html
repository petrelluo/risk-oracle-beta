Key Terms Glossary

Vocabulary grouped by board topic. The slide deck and guided notes call these out as "terms you NEED to know."

Thinking Skills incl. ind. variable & sample size

scientific method
Systematic process: observe → question → hypothesize → test → confirm/reject → incorporate.
observation
A fact obtained using your senses.
interpretation (inference)
An attempt to explain the facts.
hypothesis
A testable prediction made before the experiment. "If… then… because…"
theory
An explanation supported by repeated experiments giving the same result. Remains until disproved.
independent variable
The variable you change on purpose (x-axis).
dependent variable
The variable you measure; depends on the independent (y-axis).
control (controlled variable)
Factor held constant so it doesn't affect the result.
trial
One complete run of an experiment.
sample size
Number of trials or data points. Larger = more reliable.

Averaging & % Error

average (mean)
Sum of all values ÷ number of values.
weighted average
Σ(value × weight) / Σ(weights). Used when data points "count more" than others.
outlier
A data point noticeably different from the rest. Distorts averages in small samples.
accuracy
How close your value is to the accepted (theoretical) value.
precision
How close repeated measurements are to each other.
theoretical value
The accepted or true value. Also called "theoretical yield."
experimental value
What you got in lab. Also called "experimental yield."
percent error
|theoretical − experimental| / theoretical × 100 — exactly as the teacher wrote it on the board.

Lab Equipment & Science Communication

graduated cylinder
Precision glassware for measuring liquid volume. Read at the bottom of the meniscus.
beaker
For mixing/holding liquids. Volume marks are approximate only.
Erlenmeyer flask
Cone-shaped flask with a narrow neck; good for swirling without splashing.
volumetric flask
Pear-shaped flask with a single line marking — used to prepare a solution of exact concentration.
burette / pipette
Precision glassware for delivering specific volumes (used in titrations and dilutions).
ring stand
Vertical metal rod holding clamps, rings, and apparatus during experiments.
Bunsen burner
Gas burner that produces a controlled flame for heating.
balance
Device for measuring mass. Tare/zero before each use.
thermometer
Measures temperature; bulb should contact only the material being measured.
lab report
Standardized write-up: title, purpose, hypothesis, materials, procedure, data, analysis, conclusion, sources of error.
sources of error
Equipment limitations or procedural issues — NOT "human error" per the slides.

Separation Techniques

filtration
Separates an insoluble solid from a liquid using filter paper (e.g., sand from water).
distillation
Separates liquids based on different boiling points. Vapor is condensed and collected (e.g., salt water → pure water).
evaporation
Removes a liquid solvent to leave a dissolved solid behind (e.g., salt from salt water).
chromatography
Separates components of a mixture by how fast each moves through a stationary medium (e.g., separating ink dyes).
decanting
Pouring off a liquid carefully, leaving a solid (denser material) behind. Quick and rough.
separating funnel
Separates two immiscible liquids of different densities (e.g., oil and water).
magnetism
Separates a magnetic substance (iron) from a non-magnetic one (sand).
centrifugation
Rapid spinning separates substances of different densities (used in biology too).
distillate
The condensed liquid collected after distillation.

Making Solutions

solution
A homogeneous mixture made by dissolving one substance in another.
solute
The substance being dissolved (usually present in smaller amount). Sugar in lemonade.
solvent
The substance doing the dissolving (usually larger amount). Water in lemonade.
aqueous solution
A solution where the solvent is water (abbreviated aq).
concentration
How much solute is dissolved per amount of solution. Expressed as % m/m, % v/v, or molarity (M).
concentrated solution
A relatively large amount of dissolved solute.
dilute solution
A relatively small amount of dissolved solute.
dilution
Adding more solvent to lower the concentration. Moles of solute stay the same.
stock solution
A high-concentration solution kept for later dilution into working solutions.
saturated
A solution holding the maximum solute it can at that temperature.

Drawing Perspectives

scientific drawing
A neat, labeled sketch of what you observe — captures size, shape, and structure without artistic embellishment.
front view (elevation)
What you see looking directly at the object from the front.
side view
What you see from the left or right side (profile).
top view (bird's eye / plan)
What you see looking down on the object from above.
isometric / 3-D perspective
Shows three faces at once at an angle — useful for showing depth.
cross-section
The "inside" view as if the object were cut in half.
scale bar / label
Indicator of size and identifying labels on a scientific drawing.

Measurement Vocabulary Slides & guided notes

significant figures
All certain digits + one estimated.
captive zeros
Zeros between non-zeros (102) — always significant.
leading zeros
Zeros before the first non-zero (0.025) — never significant.
trailing zeros
Zeros after the last non-zero — significant only if a decimal point is present.
meniscus
Curved surface of a liquid. Water: concave, read bottom. Mercury: convex, read top.
parallax error
Reading from an angle gives the wrong value — always read at eye level.
conversion factor
An equivalence ratio used to cancel units in dimensional analysis.
density
D = m/V. Units g/mL or g/cm³. The slope of a mass-vs-volume graph.
direct relationship
Variables change in the same direction → straight line, y = kx.
indirect (inverse) relationship
Variables change in opposite directions → curve, xy = k.
§ 01

Thinking Skills BOARD

Board explicitly calls out independent variable and sample size under this heading.

Observation vs. Interpretation

Observation = a fact from your senses or an instrument. Interpretation = an explanation of the facts.

StatementTypeWhy
The match burnsObservationYou see flame
The match reacts with oxygenInterpretationExplaining the cause
The metal gets warmerObservationYou feel/measure it
Heat transfers to the metalInterpretationExplaining the process
Black soot is deposited on the metalObservationYou see it
The truck hit the carInterpretationYou're inferring

The Scientific Method

  1. Observe something happening
  2. Think about why — form a question
  3. Form a testable hypothesis
  4. Test — change only one variable at a time
  5. Confirm or reject
  6. Incorporate the new knowledge

Variables

Variables Cheat-Sheet Independent variable (IV) = the one you change on purpose. Goes on the x-axis.
Dependent variable (DV) = the one you measure. It "depends on" the IV. Goes on the y-axis.
Control = factors held constant so they don't influence the DV.
Memory trick: "I change the I; I measure the D."

Sample Size

  • Larger sample size = more reliable results. Random errors tend to cancel out.
  • One trial is not enough — a single measurement could be a fluke.
  • Small samples are vulnerable to outliers.
  • Rule of thumb in chem lab: at least 3 trials before averaging.
  • Bigger sample size → better estimate of both the true value and the precision.
§ 02

Averaging & Weighted Averaging BOARD

Simple Average (Mean)

x̄ = sum of all valuesnumber of values
Example
Trial masses: 5.21 g, 5.19 g, 5.23 g, 5.22 g
(5.21 + 5.19 + 5.23 + 5.22) / 4 = 20.85 / 4 = 5.21 g

Apply sig fig rules to your average — don't report more decimals than the data justifies.

Weighted Average

Use when some data points "count more" — different abundances, different trial counts, different reliabilities.

x̄weighted = Σ (value × weight)Σ weights
Classic chemistry — atomic mass of chlorine
Cl-35: 75.77 % abundance, mass 34.97 amu
Cl-37: 24.23 % abundance, mass 36.97 amu

(34.97 × 0.7577) + (36.97 × 0.2423) = 26.50 + 8.957 ≈ 35.45 amu

That's why chlorine's atomic mass is ~35.45, not a whole number.
Lab example — different trial counts
Group A: 5 trials, average 4.20 g/mL. Group B: 2 trials, average 4.50 g/mL.
(4.20 × 5 + 4.50 × 2) / (5 + 2) = 30.0 / 7 = 4.29 g/mL
A simple mean of (4.20, 4.50) would give 4.35 — wrong, because Group A had more trials.
Spot the difference If every data point contributed the same weight (e.g., one trial each) → simple average.
If they contributed different weights (abundances, trial counts, % composition) → weighted average.
§ 03

% Error BOARD

The board explicitly writes: % error = ( |Th − Exp| / Th ) × 100. Use absolute value.

% Error = theoretical − experimentaltheoretical × 100
  • theoretical value (Th) = accepted/true value
  • experimental value (Exp) = what you measured in lab
  • Always positive when you use absolute value (which the board does)

Worked examples

Calibration weight
A 100.00 g weight reads 101.41 g.
|100.00 − 101.41| / 100.00 × 100 = 1.41 %
Density of nickel
Experimental 7.59 g/mL, true 8.91 g/mL.
|8.91 − 7.59| / 8.91 × 100 = 14.8 %
Volume of a mole of gas
Bobby got 18.7 L; should be 22.4 L.
|22.4 − 18.7| / 22.4 × 100 = 16.5 %

% Recovered (Yield)

% Recovered = actualexpected × 100

Example: recovered 12.56 g of 13.00 g expected → 12.56/13.00 × 100 = 96.6 % recovered, so % error = 3.4 %.

Causes of error — slide 15 1. Limitations of the equipment (precision of the balance, thermometer, etc.)
2. NOT "human error" — the slide is explicit: "if you make a mistake, fix it!"
§ 04

Accuracy vs. Precision BOARD

  • Accuracy = how close to the bullseye (the accepted value).
  • Precision = how tightly grouped your repeated measurements are.
Dartboard patternPrecisionAccuracy
Scattered, far from bullseye✗✗
Tightly clustered, far from bullseye✓✗
Spread out, but average near bullseye✗✓
Tightly clustered on bullseye✓✓
Tell-tale pattern High precision + low accuracy = systematic error. The instrument is consistent, but consistently wrong (e.g., a miscalibrated balance reads 0.5 g too high every time).

Communication principles

  • Always include units on measured and calculated values.
  • Report numbers to the correct sig figs for the instrument used.
  • Label graph axes with the quantity and its units.
  • Use scientific notation for very large or very small numbers.
  • Draw a best-fit line through data points — never connect-the-dots.

§ 08

Making Solutions BOARD

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent.

Key vocabulary

  • Solute = what you're dissolving (smaller amount). Sugar in lemonade.
  • Solvent = what does the dissolving (larger amount). Water in lemonade.
  • Aqueous solution = solute dissolved in water (most common in chem).
  • Concentration = how much solute per amount of solution.
  • Concentrated = lots of solute relative to solvent.
  • Dilute = small amount of solute relative to solvent.
  • Saturated = no more solute can dissolve at that temperature.
  • Stock solution = a concentrated solution kept on hand for later dilution.

How to prepare a solution from a solid (general procedure)

  1. Calculate the mass of solute needed for the target concentration and volume.
  2. Weigh the solute on a tared balance.
  3. Transfer the solute into a volumetric flask (the precise glassware).
  4. Add some solvent and swirl to dissolve.
  5. Fill the flask up to the etched line with more solvent.
  6. Mix thoroughly by inverting and swirling.
  7. Label with the chemical name, concentration, and date.

Why a volumetric flask?

A volumetric flask has a single line for the final volume — far more precise than a beaker. You don't measure the solvent separately; you dissolve the solute and then bring the total volume up to the line.

Dilution

Adding more solvent decreases concentration without changing the amount of solute. If you ever see the equation:

M1V1 = M2V2

that's the dilution formula (initial molarity × initial volume = final molarity × final volume). Likely beyond MQ2B — but recognize that moles of solute stay constant during a dilution.

Safety When making acid solutions: always add Acid to Water, never the reverse. ("Do as you oughta — add acid to water.")
§ 09

Drawing Perspectives BOARD

Scientific drawing is about capturing exactly what you see — clearly, accurately, with labels — from the perspective that best shows the structure.

Standard perspectives

PerspectiveWhat it showsWhen to use it
Front view (elevation)Object as seen face-onMost common — shows height & width clearly
Side view (profile)Object from the left or rightShows depth, layering
Top view (bird's-eye / plan)Looking down from aboveShows arrangement, footprint, internal grid
Isometric / 3-DThree faces at once at an angleShows overall 3-D shape
Cross-sectionThe "inside" cut openShows internal structure (e.g., a layered solution)

Rules for a good scientific drawing

  1. Use a sharp pencil — never a pen — so corrections are possible.
  2. Draw what you see, not what you think you should see. Don't add details that aren't there.
  3. Label everything. Use straight lines (not arrows) and write labels horizontally.
  4. Include a scale bar or note the actual size so the drawing has context.
  5. Use clean, single lines — no sketchy, fuzzy multi-line strokes.
  6. Don't shade with pencil — use stippling (dots) or hatching if you need to show shadow.
  7. Title the drawing with what it shows and the perspective used.

Why this matters for chemistry labs

  • Drawings of apparatus setups (distillation, filtration) show that you understand what equipment connects to what.
  • Drawings of before vs after capture observations in a way text cannot (e.g., a colorless solution turning blue with a precipitate).
  • Drawings of layered liquids in a separating funnel show density relationships at a glance.
Quick guidance For an apparatus setup, the front view is almost always the right choice — that's the angle from which you operate the equipment.
For a layered solution (e.g., oil over water), draw a side view of the container so you can label each layer clearly.

Significant Figures 2.8

Counting sig figs

  1. All integers (1–9) are significant.
  2. Captive zeros (between non-zeros, like 102) — always significant.
  3. Leading zeros (0.0025) — never significant.
  4. Trailing zeros — significant only if there's a decimal point.
    • 120 → 2 sig figs · 120. → 3 sig figs · 120.0 → 4 sig figs

Density & Graphing 2.3/2.2

D = mV

Rearranged: m = D × V and V = m / D.

Baseball
V = 210 cm³, D = 0.71 g/cm³ → m = 0.71 × 210 = 149 g
Copper block (water displacement)
Water rises 20.0 → 25.9 mL, so V = 5.9 mL. D = 8.96 g/mL → m = 52.9 g

Graphing relationships

DirectIndirect (inverse)
As x ↑y ↑y ↓
ShapeStraight lineCurve approaching axes
Equationy = kx (k = slope)xy = k

Slope = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁). On a mass vs. volume graph, the slope = density.

§ 15

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Mixing up accuracy and precision. Accuracy = bullseye. Precision = grouping.
  • Forgetting absolute value in % error (the board uses |Th − Exp|).
  • Citing "human error" as a source of error. The slides explicitly say don't.
  • Mixing up solute and solvent. Solute is what dissolves; solvent does the dissolving.
  • Using a beaker for precise volume. Beakers are approximate — use a graduated cylinder or volumetric flask.
  • Adding water to acid. Always do the reverse: Acid to Water.
  • Reading from an angle → parallax error.
  • Connecting graph data dot-to-dot instead of a best-fit line.
  • Picking distillation for separating sand from water. That's filtration.
  • Using a simple mean when the data points have different weights.
  • Mistaking 120 for 120. The decimal point changes the sig figs!
  • Forgetting to label axes with quantity AND units.

Walk in confident. Read carefully. Check sig figs at the end.
