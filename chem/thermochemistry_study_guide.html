01

Collision Theory & Reaction Rates

Why don't all molecular collisions result in a reaction?

Molecules must collide to react — but only a small fraction of collisions actually produces a reaction. Two conditions must both be met:

  • Correct orientation — the molecules must be arranged so that new bonds can form. If the geometry is wrong, the collision bounces off with "no reaction."
  • Sufficient energy — the collision must carry at least the activation energy (Ea), the minimum energy needed to break the existing bonds in the reactants.

Four factors that speed up a reaction

FactorWhy it speeds up the rate
Temperature ↑Particles move faster → more collisions, and more of them exceed Ea.
Surface area ↑Smaller particles expose more surface → more places to collide.
Concentration ↑More particles present → more collisions possible.
CatalystLowers the activation energy → more particles already have enough energy to react.

Definitions

Activation energy: the minimum amount of energy required to break existing bonds so a reaction can proceed.

Catalyst: a substance that lowers the activation energy required for a reaction — without being consumed itself.

Key Idea

A reaction rate increases by raising temperature, surface area, or concentration (all give more collisions), or by adding a catalyst to lower the activation-energy barrier — which lets more molecules carry enough energy to break reactant bonds when they collide.

02

Energy Diagrams & Enthalpy

What determines whether energy is absorbed or released during a reaction?

Energy changes over the course of every reaction. Energy is absorbed from the surroundings to break reactant bonds (the climb to the activation-energy peak), and energy is released to the surroundings as new product bonds form (the drop down the other side).

Comparing the total enthalpy of the products to that of the reactants gives the overall enthalpy of reaction, ΔHrxn.

TypeHeat flowSign of ΔHProducts vs reactants
EndothermicAbsorbs heat (energy input)Positive (+)Products higher in energy
ExothermicReleases heat (energy out)Negative (−)Products lower in energy

Sign convention

A positive (+) ΔH means energy is absorbed from the surroundings.

A negative (−) ΔH means energy is released to the surroundings.

Effect of a catalyst on the diagram: a catalyst lowers only the activation-energy peak (a lower hump). It does not change the energy of the reactants or products, so ΔHrxn stays exactly the same.

Worked Example — reading a diagram

Given a diagram where reactants sit at 80 kJ, the activation peak reaches 120 kJ, and products rest at 40 kJ:

Activation energy = peak − reactants = 120 − 80 = 40 kJ
Energy released as products form = peak − products = 120 − 40 = 80 kJ
ΔHrxn = products − reactants = 40 − 80
ΔHrxn = −40 kJ → exothermic
03

Bond Energy & ΔHrxn

Predict whether a reaction is endothermic or exothermic using bond energies.

Definition

Bond energy: the energy required to break a chemical bond — equal to the energy released when that same bond forms. The more energy it takes to break a bond, the stronger and more stable that bond is.

Breaking bonds costs energy (endothermic, +). Forming bonds releases energy (exothermic, −). The net is:

ΔHrxn = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed)

Worked Example 1 — formation of water

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O  |  H–H = 432, O=O = 495, O–H = 467 kJ/mol

Bonds broken (input): 2(H–H) + 1(O=O) = 2(432) + 495 = 1359 kJ
Bonds formed (output): 4 O–H bonds = 4(467) = 1868 kJ
ΔH = 1359 − 1868
ΔHrxn = −509 kJ → exothermic (energy released)
Worked Example 2 — hydrogenation

C2H2 + 2H2 → C2H6  |  H–H = 432, C–C = 347, C–H = 413, C≡C = 839 kJ/mol

Bonds broken: C≡C + 2(C–H) + 2(H–H) = 839 + 826 + 864 = 2529 kJ
Bonds formed: C–C + 6(C–H) = 347 + 2478 = 2825 kJ
ΔH = 2529 − 2825
ΔHrxn = −296 kJ → exothermic (ΔH is negative, so energy is released)

Key Idea

If more energy is needed to break bonds than is released forming new bonds, the reaction is endothermic and ΔH is positive. If forming bonds releases more energy than breaking them costs, the reaction is exothermic and ΔH is negative.

04

Heating & Cooling Curves

Why doesn't the temperature change during a phase change?

As heat is added, temperature rises on the sloped segments (one phase warming) and stays flat on the plateaus (a phase change in progress).

On a heating curve, two kinds of segments alternate:

  • Sloped segments (rising temperature): a single phase is warming. Particles move faster, average kinetic energy rises, temperature rises.
  • Flat plateaus (constant temperature): a phase change is happening. All added energy goes into overcoming intermolecular forces (potential energy), so temperature stays constant.

The flat regions answer the essential question: during a phase change, energy doesn't speed the particles up — it pulls them apart. The melting plateau and the boiling plateau both occur at a single fixed temperature.

Melting vs. boiling: why the boiling plateau is longer

The boiling (vaporization) plateau is longer than the melting (fusion) plateau because it takes more energy to fully separate particles into a gas than to merely let them flow as a liquid. Vaporization must overcome intermolecular forces almost completely.

Same temperature, opposite directionEnergy
Melting ↔ Freezing (same temp)Melting absorbs heat; freezing releases heat
Boiling ↔ Condensing (same temp)Boiling absorbs heat; condensing releases heat

Key Idea

As energy is added, particles move faster → higher average kinetic energy → higher temperature.

During a phase change, added energy breaks intermolecular forces instead, so the temperature remains constant.

05

Specific Heat — Q = cmΔT

What is heat, and how do we calculate it?

Heat and temperature are not the same. Heat is the transfer of kinetic energy from a hotter substance to a colder one; temperature is the average kinetic energy of particles.

Definition

Specific heat capacity (c): the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 degree. Typical units: J/(g·°C) or J/(g·K).

Q = c · m · ΔT

SymbolMeaningUnit
Q (or q)Heat flowJ
cSpecific heatJ/(g·°C)
mMassg
ΔTTemperature change (Tfinal − Tinitial)°C or K

Rearrangements:   c = Q / (mΔT)  •  m = Q / (cΔT)  •  ΔT = Q / (mc)

Specific heats of common substances

Substancec, J/(g·°C)
Water (liquid)4.18
Water (solid / ice)2.10
Aluminum (s)0.897
Iron (s)0.439
Copper (s)0.385
Lead (s)0.129

A low specific heat means a substance heats up (and cools) quickly — metals like iron and copper. Water's high specific heat (4.18) means it resists temperature change, needing lots of energy per gram per degree.

Worked Example 1 — solve for c

4.0 g of glass warms from 274 °C to 314 °C by absorbing 32.0 J. Find c.

ΔT = 314 − 274 = 40 °C
c = Q / (mΔT) = 32.0 / (4.0 × 40)
c = 0.20 J/(g·°C)
Worked Example 2 — solve for Q (and a units lesson)

That same glass (c = 0.20) cools from 325 K to 275 K. How much heat is released?

ΔT = 50 K = 50 °C  (a change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C, so ΔT need not be converted)
Q = cmΔT = 0.20 × 4.0 × 50
Q = 40 J released

Key Idea

Heat is absorbed or released because of a temperature difference. Every substance has its own ability to absorb heat (its specific heat). Metals have low specific heat and heat up fast; water has a high specific heat and resists temperature change.

06

Multi-Step Problems with Phase Changes

Moving across a whole heating curve — including the flat plateaus.

To heat a substance through phase changes, you add up the energy for every segment. Sloped segments use Q = cmΔT; flat plateaus use the latent heat of fusion (melting) or vaporization (boiling), which have no ΔT term.

Qtotal = mcsΔT + mΔHfus + mclΔT + mΔHvap + mcgΔT

SegmentWhat's happeningEquation
1 · Solid warmingIce rising to its melting pointm · csolid · ΔT
2 · Melting (plateau)Solid → liquid, constant Tm · ΔHfusion
3 · Liquid warmingLiquid rising to its boiling pointm · cliquid · ΔT
4 · Boiling (plateau)Liquid → gas, constant Tm · ΔHvaporization
5 · Gas warmingVapor heating furtherm · cgas · ΔT

Strategy: figure out where on the curve you start and end. Only include the segments you actually cross. The plateau terms (fusion, vaporization) appear only when the substance fully changes phase during the problem.

07

Calorimetry

How is heat actually measured? It's all about the water.

We can't measure the total heat energy of a system, but we can measure the change. Calorimetry relies on the second law of thermodynamics: heat flows naturally from the hotter object to the colder object. The heat lost by one equals the heat gained by the other:

−Qblock = Qwater   or   cxmxΔTx = cymyΔTy

Definitions

Calorimetry: measuring the heat absorbed or released by a reaction or process.

System: the reaction or process being studied.

Surroundings: everything else — usually the water in the calorimeter.

Reading the direction: if the water heats up, the system released heat → reaction is exothermic, ΔH is negative. If the water cools down, the system absorbed heat → endothermic, ΔH is positive.

Worked Example — specific heat of a metal

A 59.047 g metal heated to 100.0 °C is dropped into 100.0 mL water at 23.7 °C. Equilibrium = 27.8 °C. Find cmetal.

Step 1 — heat gained by water: Qw = 100.0 g × 4.18 × (27.8 − 23.7) = +1714 J
Step 2 — that heat came from the metal: Qmetal = −1714 J
c = Q / (mΔT) = −1714 / [59.047 × (27.8 − 100.0)]
cmetal ≈ 0.40 J/(g·°C)

Coffee-cup vs. bomb calorimeter — a crucial difference

Coffee-cup (constant pressure)Bomb (constant volume)
Reaction happens…in the water (mixed in)sealed away from the water
Mass used in Q = cmΔTTotal contents (water + dissolved reactants)Water mass only
Typical useSolution reactions, dissolvingCombustion reactions
Worked Example — coffee-cup (CaO + H₂O)

19.3 g CaO (56.1 g/mol) is added to 130 g water; the mixture warms 22.0 → 57.2 °C. Find ΔHrxn.

Water heated up → reaction released heat. Mass = total contents = 19.3 + 130 = 149.3 g
ΔT = 57.2 − 22.0 = 35.2 °C
Q = 4.18 × 149.3 × 35.2 = 21,967 J → ΔHrxn = −21,967 J
Per mole: moles CaO = 19.3 / 56.1 = 0.344 mol
ΔHrxn ≈ −63.9 kJ/mol CaO  (values must be per-mole or per-gram to compare reactions)
Worked Example — bomb (combustion of methane)

1.44 g CH₄ combusts in a bomb calorimeter with 500 g water; T rises 20.0 → 35.6 °C. Find ΔH in kJ/mol.

Bomb → use water mass only. ΔT = 15.6 °C
Qw = 4.18 × 500 × 15.6 = 32,604 J = 32.604 kJ → ΔHrxn = −32.604 kJ
moles CH₄ = 1.44 / 16.0 = 0.0898 mol
ΔHcombustion = −32.604 / 0.0898 ≈ −363 kJ/mol CH₄

Key Idea

ΔHrxn is measured with calorimetry. If the water warms, the system released energy (exothermic, ΔH negative). If the water cools, the system absorbed energy (endothermic, ΔH positive). Always report ΔH per mole or per gram so reactions can be compared.

Free-Response Challenge

Try these on paper, then reveal the worked answer.

1. A 250.0 g block of copper (c = 0.385) cools from 95.0 °C to 25.0 °C. How much heat does it release?
Show answer
ΔT = 25.0 − 95.0 = −70.0 °C.   Q = cmΔT = 0.385 × 250.0 × (−70.0) = −6738 J ≈ −6.74 kJ (released).
2. A 75 g metal at 100.0 °C is placed in 150.0 g water at 20.0 °C; equilibrium is 24.5 °C. Find the metal's specific heat.
Show answer
Qwater = 4.18 × 150.0 × (24.5 − 20.0) = +2821.5 J. So Qmetal = −2821.5 J.   c = Q / (mΔT) = −2821.5 / [75 × (24.5 − 100.0)] = −2821.5 / (−5662.5) = ≈ 0.498 J/(g·°C).
3. Identify each as endothermic or exothermic, and whether the surroundings gain or lose heat: (a) an iron pan heated on a stove, (b) an athlete taking an ice bath, (c) ice melting in a glass of water.
Show answer
(a) The stove puts energy into the pan → endothermic (for the pan); surroundings lose heat. (b) The athlete releases energy into the ice bath → exothermic; surroundings gain heat. (c) The water puts energy into the ice (solid→liquid) → endothermic; surroundings lose heat.
