Core concepts

What stoichiometry is, and the framework that powers every problem.

Stoichiometry is the calculation of amounts of substances — reactants consumed or products formed — in a chemical reaction. Every problem in this unit reduces to the same three-step framework.

STEP 01Get to moles

Convert the substance you're given into moles. Use molar mass for grams, Avogadro's number for particles, or 22.4 L/mol for gases at STP.

STEP 02Use the mole ratio

The coefficients of the balanced equation form the mole ratio. This is the only way to cross from one substance to another.

STEP 03Convert to the desired unit

Take moles of the new substance and convert to whatever the question asks for — grams, particles, or liters of gas.

The only way to convert between two different substances is through moles. You cannot go directly from grams of A to grams of B. Moles is the bridge — every time.

Conversion factors to memorize

ConversionFactor
Mass ↔ moles1 mole = molar mass (g)
Particles ↔ moles1 mole = 6.02 × 10²³ particles
Gas volume ↔ moles (STP)1 mole = 22.4 L
Substance A ↔ Substance Bmole ratio from balanced equation

Where mole ratios come from

Coefficients in a balanced chemical equation are both particle ratios and mole ratios — because a mole is just a fixed number of particles. For 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O:

  • Mole ratio of H₂ to O₂: 2 mol H₂ / 1 mol O₂ (or its reciprocal).
  • Mole ratio of H₂O to O₂: 2 mol H₂O / 1 mol O₂.
  • Mole ratio of H₂ to H₂O: 2 mol H₂ / 2 mol H₂O = 1 : 1.

Recipe analogy (Dr. Suzette's turkey-club; CK-12's pancakes): if a club takes 4 strips of bacon, then 2 clubs take 8. The same logic, applied to atoms, is stoichiometry.

Limiting reactant — the heart of every quiz problem

The limiting reactant (LR) is the reactant that runs out first and so limits how much product can form. The excess reactant is what's left over when the reaction stops.

Once a limiting reactant is known, it must be used for ALL stoichiometry calculations. The LR sets the ceiling for product, the theoretical yield, and how much excess gets consumed.

The trigger. Any time amounts are given for both reactants, stop and find the LR before calculating anything else. If the question says “with excess X,” ignore X's amount — the other reactant is your LR.

Method 1 — calculate product from each reactant

Pretend each reactant is the limiting one and run a full calculation. Whichever reactant gives the smaller amount of product is the LR.

Example (notes): 5.0 g Fe + 5.0 g S, with 2 Fe + 3 S → Fe₂S₃:

  • If Fe runs out first → 5.0 g Fe yields 9.31 g Fe₂S₃.
  • If S runs out first → 5.0 g S yields 10.79 g Fe₂S₃.
  • Fe gives less product → Fe is the limiting reactant.

Method 2 — “need vs. have”

Pick a reactant. Calculate how much of the other reactant it would need to react completely. Compare to what you actually have.

FeS
Initial (have)5.0 g5.0 g
Needed5.79 g4.31 g
Endnot enough — LR0.69 g leftover (excess)

Both methods always agree. Method 2 is especially handy when the question also asks how much excess is left over.

Excess reactant calculations

Once you know the LR, calculate how much of the excess reactant actually reacted by starting from the LR amount and using the mole ratio:

amount of LR  →  mol LR  →  mol excess  →  grams excess

Then: leftover = initial amount − amount that reacted.

Example (notes): 3.50 g Al + 4.0 g O₂ with 4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃. The 3.50 g Al would need 3.11 g O₂; we have 4.0 g, so Al is the LR. Leftover O₂ = 4.0 − 3.11 = 0.89 g.

Theoretical yield, experimental yield, percent yield

TermMeaning
Theoretical yieldAmount of product that should form per stoichiometry, assuming 100% conversion. Calculated from the LR.
Experimental yieldAmount of product actually obtained in the lab. Given to you.
Percent yieldEfficiency of the reaction.
percent yield  =  experimental yield theoretical yield  ×  100

Always use the same units for experimental and theoretical (usually grams).

Why actual yield falls below theoretical

  • Reactants may not be 100% pure.
  • Material is inevitably lost in transfer (you can't get every drop out of the flask).
  • At high temperature, some product may vaporize.
  • Side reactions may consume reactants in unexpected ways.

Worked example (notes)

10.00 g Fe reacts with excess S to form 16.50 g Fe₂S₃ (2 Fe + 3 S → Fe₂S₃). Theoretical: 18.62 g. Actual: 16.50 g. Percent yield = (16.50 / 18.62) × 100 = 88.6%.

Problem-solving checklist

  1. Balance the equation. Without correct coefficients everything else is wrong.
  2. Identify what's given (substance + unit) and what's wanted (substance + unit).
  3. If amounts of both reactants are given, find the LR before going further.
  4. Get to moles of the given substance.
  5. Apply the mole ratio.
  6. Convert to the desired unit of the target substance.
  7. Check sig figs and units.

Quiz I — concept & short calculation

Ten questions. Try them all without peeking. Answers are at the very end of this guide.

Multiple choice

  1. In a chemical reaction, the limiting reactant is best described as:
    1. The reactant present in the largest amount.
    2. The reactant that runs out first and limits how much product forms.
    3. The reactant with the smallest molar mass.
    4. The reactant left over after the reaction stops.
  2. “Once a limiting reactant is known, it MUST be used for ALL stoichiometry calculations.” This is true because:
    1. The excess reactant doesn't really react at all.
    2. The LR determines the maximum amount of product that can form.
    3. The LR always has the largest coefficient.
    4. The LR always has the smallest molar mass.
  3. Which conversion can NOT be done directly without going through moles?
    1. Grams of A → moles of A.
    2. Moles of B → particles of B.
    3. Grams of A → grams of B.
    4. Moles of A → moles of B (using the mole ratio).
  4. The percent yield formula is:
    1. (theoretical / experimental) × 100
    2. (experimental / theoretical) × 100
    3. theoretical − experimental
    4. experimental + theoretical
  5. Which of the following is NOT a typical reason actual yield is less than theoretical yield?
    1. Side reactions consume some of the reactants.
    2. Material is lost during transfer.
    3. The balanced equation is wrong.
    4. Some product vaporizes at high temperature.

Short calculations

  1. Given 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, how many moles of H₂O are produced from 0.75 mol of O₂ reacting with excess H₂?
  2. For 4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃, how many moles of Al are needed to react completely with 6.0 mol of O₂?
  3. In 2 Fe + 3 S → Fe₂S₃, calculate the mass of Fe₂S₃ formed when 6.50 g of Fe reacts with excess S. (Fe = 55.85 g/mol; Fe₂S₃ = 207.9 g/mol.)
  4. For N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃, a chemist mixes 3 mol N₂ with 6 mol H₂. Which is the limiting reactant, and how many moles of NH₃ form? (The CK-12 Haber-process example.)
  5. A reaction has a theoretical yield of 18.62 g of product. The student measures 16.50 g in the lab. What is the percent yield?

Quiz II — multi-step problems

Mirrors the example boxes from the guided notes. Show every conversion factor — don't skip the mole-ratio line.

  3. Sodium azide decomposes in airbags: 2 NaN₃ → 2 Na + 3 N₂. How many liters of N₂ at STP are produced from 130 g of NaN₃? (NaN₃ = 65.02 g/mol; 22.4 L/mol at STP.)

    2. Calculate the percent yield.
  5. Silver reacts with sulfur: 2 Ag + S → Ag₂S (CK-12 example). 50.0 g Ag is reacted with 10.0 g S. (Ag = 107.87 g/mol; S = 32.07 g/mol.)

Pocket cheat sheet

Glance at this five minutes before the quiz starts.

The 3-step framework

given units  →  moles  →  mole ratio  →  moles  →  desired units

Conversion factors

  • 1 mol = molar mass in grams
  • 1 mol = 6.02 × 10²³ particles
  • 1 mol of gas = 22.4 L (STP only)

Limiting reactant

  • Trigger: amounts given for BOTH reactants.
  • Method 1: calculate product from each → smaller wins.
  • Method 2: need vs. have — compare what's needed to what you have.
  • Once you have the LR, use it (and only it) for theoretical yield, product mass, and excess calculations.

Excess & yield

  • Excess leftover = initial − amount that reacted.
  • Percent yield = (experimental ÷ theoretical) × 100.
  • Why <100%: impure reactants · material lost in transfer · vaporization · side reactions.

Quiz I — answer key

Q1 — B

The LR runs out first and limits product formation. The amount given doesn't matter on its own; the ratio compared to the equation does.

Q2 — B

The LR sets the ceiling on product. The excess does react — just not all of it.

Q3 — C

You cannot go from grams of one substance to grams of another without passing through moles. Moles is the bridge between substances.

Q4 — B

(experimental ÷ theoretical) × 100.

Q5 — C

An unbalanced equation would be a calculation error, not a reason yield is low. The other three are real lab causes.

Q6 — 1.5 mol H₂O

0.75 mol O₂ × (2 mol H₂O / 1 mol O₂) = 1.5 mol H₂O.

Q7 — 8.0 mol Al

6.0 mol O₂ × (4 mol Al / 3 mol O₂) = 8.0 mol Al.

Q8 — 12.1 g Fe₂S₃

6.50 g Fe × (1 mol Fe / 55.85 g) × (1 mol Fe₂S₃ / 2 mol Fe) × (207.9 g / 1 mol) = 12.1 g Fe₂S₃.

Q9 — H₂ is the LR; 4 mol NH₃ form

The recipe needs N₂ : H₂ = 1 : 3. With 3 mol N₂ you'd need 9 mol H₂, but you only have 6 — so H₂ is the LR. Using H₂: 6 mol H₂ × (2 mol NH₃ / 3 mol H₂) = 4 mol NH₃. 1 mol N₂ left unreacted.

Q10 — 88.6%

(16.50 / 18.62) × 100 = 88.6%.

Quiz II — answer key

Q1 — 4.0 mol Al + 4.0 mol O₂

Find LR (Method 2):

  • 4.0 mol Al × (3 mol O₂ / 4 mol Al) = 3.0 mol O₂ needed. We have 4.0 mol → enough.
  • 4.0 mol O₂ × (4 mol Al / 3 mol O₂) = 5.33 mol Al needed. We have only 4.0 mol → not enough.

(a) Al is the limiting reactant. (b) 4.0 mol Al × (2 mol Al₂O₃ / 4 mol Al) = 2.0 mol Al₂O₃. (c) 4.0 mol O₂ initial − 3.0 mol reacted = 1.0 mol O₂ left over.

Q2 — 3.50 g Al + 4.0 g O₂

Need-vs-have:

  • 3.50 g Al × (1 mol / 26.98 g) × (3 mol O₂ / 4 mol Al) × (32.00 g / 1 mol) = 3.11 g O₂ needed. We have 4.0 g → enough.
  • 4.0 g O₂ × (1 mol / 32.00 g) × (4 mol Al / 3 mol O₂) × (26.98 g / 1 mol) = 4.50 g Al needed. We have only 3.50 g → not enough.

(a) Al is the limiting reactant. (b) Excess O₂ = 4.0 − 3.11 = 0.89 g O₂. (c) 3.50 g Al × (1 mol / 26.98 g) × (2 mol Al₂O₃ / 4 mol Al) × (101.96 g / 1 mol) = 6.61 g Al₂O₃.

Q3 — 67.2 L N₂

130 g NaN₃ × (1 mol / 65.02 g) × (3 mol N₂ / 2 mol NaN₃) × (22.4 L / 1 mol) = 67.2 L N₂.

Q4 — 6.30 g Al → 11.10 g Al₂O₃ actual

(a) Theoretical: 6.30 g Al × (1 mol / 26.98 g) × (2 mol Al₂O₃ / 4 mol Al) × (101.96 g / 1 mol) = 11.90 g Al₂O₃.

(b) Percent yield = (11.10 / 11.90) × 100 = 93.3% (≈ 93.2% with intermediate rounding — both accepted).

Q5 — 50.0 g Ag + 10.0 g S

Convert to moles: 50.0 g Ag × (1 mol / 107.87 g) = 0.464 mol Ag; 10.0 g S × (1 mol / 32.07 g) = 0.312 mol S.

S needed for all the Ag: 0.464 mol Ag × (1 mol S / 2 mol Ag) = 0.232 mol S. We have 0.312 mol — more than enough.

(a) Ag is the limiting reactant; S is in excess.

(b) The required Ag : S mole ratio is 2 : 1. We have 0.464 mol Ag and 0.312 mol S — that's less than a 2 : 1 ratio of Ag to S, so Ag runs out first. Equivalently, only 0.232 mol of the available S is needed, leaving 0.080 mol S unreacted.

Quiz III — answer key

Q1 — Ethanol combustion (4.0 mol C₂H₅OH)

Mole ratios from C₂H₅OH + 3 O₂ → 2 CO₂ + 3 H₂O:

  • (a) 4.0 mol × (3 mol O₂ / 1) = 12 mol O₂.
  • (b) 4.0 mol × (2 mol CO₂ / 1) = 8.0 mol CO₂.
  • (c) 4.0 mol × (3 mol H₂O / 1) = 12 mol H₂O.

Q2 — 173 g H₂O

9.6 mol H₂ × (2 mol H₂O / 2 mol H₂) × (18.02 g / 1 mol) = 173 g H₂O (≈ 1.7 × 10² g).

Q3 — 1.88 × 10²³ molecules CO₂

5.00 g CH₄ × (1 mol / 16.04 g) × (1 mol CO₂ / 1 mol CH₄) × (6.02 × 10²³ molecules / 1 mol) = 1.88 × 10²³ molecules CO₂.

Q4 — 8.00 g Fe + 6.00 g S

Convert: 8.00 g Fe × (1/55.85) = 0.1432 mol Fe; 6.00 g S × (1/32.07) = 0.1871 mol S.

S needed to fully react Fe: 0.1432 × (3 S / 2 Fe) = 0.2148 mol S. We have only 0.1871 → not enough S.

(a) S is the limiting reactant.

(b) Use S for product: 0.1871 mol S × (1 mol Fe₂S₃ / 3 mol S) × (207.9 g / 1 mol) = 13.0 g Fe₂S₃.

(c) Fe that reacted: 0.1871 mol S × (2 mol Fe / 3 mol S) × (55.85 g / 1 mol) = 6.97 g Fe. Leftover Fe = 8.00 − 6.97 = 1.03 g Fe excess.

Q5 — 85.0%

Percent yield = (42.5 / 50.0) × 100 = 85.0%.

Two reasons (any two): impure reactants; material lost during transfer; product vaporized at high temperature; side reactions occurred.

Q6 — Concept

Coefficients in a balanced equation are ratios of moles (or particles), not of grams. Two reactants with very different molar masses can have the same mass but very different numbers of moles, so comparing grams directly tells you nothing about which one runs out first. You must convert to moles to compare against the mole ratio.
