Key Terms Glossary

Every "Terms you NEED to know" from the slides and guided notes, grouped by topic. The slides explicitly call out these terms to listen for — that's your vocab list.

Thinking Skills Topic 2.1 · Slides 21–26

scientific method
The systematic process scientists use to answer questions by making observations and explaining them: observe → question → hypothesize → test → confirm/reject → incorporate.
observation
A fact obtained using your senses. No explanation attached.
interpretation (inference)
An attempt to explain the facts. Adds reasoning, cause, or judgment.
hypothesis
A testable prediction made before doing an experiment. "If… then… because…" form.
theory
An explanation suggested when multiple experiments provide the same data about a phenomenon. Theories remain in place until data is found to disprove them.
variable
A factor in an experiment that can change. Only one should be varied at a time.
independent variable
The variable you change (vary) on purpose. Goes on the x-axis.
dependent variable
The variable you measure — its value depends on the independent variable. Goes on the y-axis.
control (controlled variable)
Any factor held constant during the experiment so it doesn't affect the result.

Density Topic 2.3 · Slides 5–10

density
How the mass of a substance compares to its volume. D = m/V. Units: g/mL (liquids) or g/cm³ (solids).
mass
The amount of matter in an object. Measured in grams (g) on a balance.
volume
The amount of space an object occupies. Measured in mL or cm³ (1 mL = 1 cm³).

Metric System Topic 2.4 · Slides 27–35

metric system
The international system of measurement based on powers of 10. Units scale by factors of 10, 100, 1000.
base unit
The "starting" unit for a quantity, with no prefix attached. Gram (mass), liter (volume), meter (length), second (time).
prefix
A modifier (kilo, centi, milli, micro, nano, pico) attached to a base unit. The most-used in chemistry are kilo- (1000× larger) and milli- (1000× smaller). Micro and nano are used for small things like atoms and electrons.

Making Measurements Topic 2.5 · Slides 37–42

graduated
Marked with a series of lines (graduations) to allow measurement.
meniscus
The curved surface of a liquid in a narrow container. Water forms a concave meniscus (read the bottom); mercury forms a convex one (read the top).
parallax error
The reading error caused by viewing a measurement not at eye level. Read from higher → measurement appears lower; read from lower → measurement appears higher.
known (certain) digits
The digits in a measurement that you read directly from the instrument's marked lines.
estimated digit
The last digit in a measurement — your best guess of the value between the smallest marked lines (visually divide into 10 parts). Only one estimated digit.
uncertainty
The unavoidable "fuzziness" in any measurement — instruments cannot read to an infinite number of decimal places.

Dimensional Analysis Topic 2.6 · Slides 52–57

dimensional analysis
A method for converting between units using conversion factors, set up so that unwanted units cancel out. Also called the factor-label method.
conversion factor
An equivalence relating two different units, used as a ratio. Examples: (1 m / 100 cm), (60 s / 1 min), (1 in / 2.54 cm).
imperial system
The non-metric system used mostly in the U.S. Inches, feet, miles, pounds, ounces, gallons.

Accuracy & % Error Topic 2.7 · Slides 11–19

accuracy
The experimental data matches the accepted value — how close you got to the bullseye.
precise (precision)
The data points are repeatable — grouped together.
theoretical value
The accepted or universally accepted value. Sometimes called "theoretical yield."
experimental value
What you get in lab. Sometimes called "experimental yield."
percent error
The difference between accepted and experimental values, as a percentage. (theoretical − experimental) / theoretical × 100.
positive error
Experimental value is LESS than theoretical.
negative error
Experimental value is GREATER than theoretical.

Precision & Significant Figures Topic 2.8 · Slides 58–64

significant figures (digits)
All the digits known for certain, plus one estimated digit. They reflect the precision of the instrument used.
captive zeros
Zeros between non-zero digits (e.g., 102). Always significant.
leading zeros
Zeros before the first non-zero digit (e.g., 0.0025). Place holders — never significant.
trailing zeros
Zeros after the last non-zero digit. Significant only if there's a decimal point.

Graphing Topic 2.2 · Slides 45–51

direct relationship
As one variable changes, the other changes in the same direction. Forms a straight line: y = kx (through origin) or y = kx + b. k is the slope.
indirect (inverse) relationship
As one variable changes one direction, the other changes the opposite direction. Forms a curve that approaches but never crosses the axes. xy = k.
slope
Steepness of a line: (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). On a mass-vs-volume graph, the slope is the density.

Data Analysis

average (mean)
Sum of all values divided by the number of values. Used to smooth out random errors across multiple trials.
weighted average
Each value is multiplied by its "weight" before summing. Σ(value × weight) / Σ(weights). Used for atomic masses of isotopes.
sample size
Number of trials or data points. Larger samples = more reliable averages.
outlier
A data point noticeably different from the rest. Distorts averages more in small samples than in large ones.

Lab Report

purpose / objective
One-sentence statement of what the experiment is trying to find or test.
procedure
Numbered list of steps — detailed enough that another scientist could repeat it.
data
Raw measurements and observations. Facts only, no interpretation.
conclusion
Interpretation: did the data support the hypothesis? How close was your value to the accepted value?
sources of error
Limitations of equipment or procedure. NOT "human error" — the slides explicitly say if you make a mistake, fix it.
§ 01

Thinking Skills 2.1

Observation vs. interpretation, the scientific method, and variables.

Observation = a fact obtained using your senses (or an instrument). No explanation, no "why."
Interpretation (inference) = an attempt to explain the facts.

Quick test: if the statement could be wrong because you're guessing why something happened, it's an interpretation.

StatementTypeWhy
The match burnsObservationYou see flame
The match reacts with oxygenInterpretationExplaining the cause
The metal gets warmerObservationYou feel/measure temperature
Heat transfers from the match to the metalInterpretationExplaining the process
Black soot is deposited on the metalObservationYou see it
There are 8 treesObservationYou can count them
The man sitting was hurtInterpretationYou don't know — you're guessing
The truck hit the carInterpretationYou're inferring what happened, not seeing it

The Scientific Method

  1. Observe something happening
  2. Think about why it's happening (form a question)
  3. Form a testable hypothesis
  4. Test your hypothesis — change only one variable at a time
  5. Confirm or reject the hypothesis
  6. Incorporate the new knowledge
Variables Vocab Independent variable = the one you change/vary (x-axis).
Dependent variable = the one you measure; it "depends" on the independent (y-axis).
Control = factors held constant so they don't affect the result.
Theory = an explanation supported by repeated experiments; stays until disproved.

§ 03

Accuracy vs. Precision 2.7

From slide 13's dartboard image. These are not the same thing.

  • Accuracy = how close a measurement is to the true / accepted / theoretical value.
  • Precision = how close repeated measurements are to each other (reproducibility).
Dartboard patternPrecisionAccuracy
Darts scattered, none near bullseye✗✗
Darts tightly clustered, but far from bullseye (slide 13 example)✓✗
Darts spread out, but average near bullseye✗✓
Darts tightly clustered on the bullseye✓✓
Watch for this High precision + low accuracy = systematic error. The instrument is consistent, but consistently wrong (e.g., a miscalibrated balance reads 0.5 g too high every time).
§ 04

% Error 2.7

% Error = theoretical − experimental theoretical × 100
  • theoretical value = accepted/true value (from a textbook or reference)
  • experimental value = what you measured in lab
Sign convention — from slide 14 Positive (+) error → experimental is LESS than theoretical.
Negative (−) error → experimental is GREATER than theoretical.
(Many teachers also accept absolute value with no sign — check the question wording.)

Worked examples (from slides 16–17)

Example 1 · Calibration weight
A student places a 100.00 g calibration weight on a balance which reads 101.41 g. What is the percent error?
(100.00 − 101.41) / 100.00 × 100 = −1.41 %
Negative because the reading was more than it should be.
Example 2 · Density of nickel
Kelly's experimental value: 7.59 g/mL. True value: 8.91 g/mL.
(8.91 − 7.59) / 8.91 × 100 = +14.8 %
Positive because she got less than the true value.
Example 3 · Volume of a mole of gas
Bobby got 18.7 L; he should have obtained 22.4 L.
(22.4 − 18.7) / 22.4 × 100 = +16.5 %
Causes of error — from slide 15 1. Limitations of the equipment (e.g., the balance only reads to 0.01 g).
2. NOT "human error" — the slide is explicit: "This is not usually cited as a source of error in a lab report… if you make a mistake, fix it!"

Related: % Recovered (Yield)

% Recovered = actual recovered expected × 100

Example: recovered 12.56 g of 13.00 g expected → 12.56/13.00 × 100 = 96.6 % recovered, so % error = 3.4 %.

Making Measurements 2.5

Reading a graduated instrument (from slide 39)

  1. Determine what value the smallest lines represent.
  2. Estimate the distance between the smallest lines by visually dividing it into 10 units.
  3. Tip: when reading the volume of a liquid, read from the bottom of the meniscus (the curve).

Other tips (slide 41)

  1. Read liquid volume at eye level to avoid parallax error.
  2. Read the bottom of the meniscus for liquids (or top for mercury).
  3. When measuring temperature, the thermometer should be in contact only with the material being measured.
  4. Make sure the bumper on a graduated cylinder is at the top to prevent breakage if it tips.

Parallax Error (slide 40)

Looking at a measurement from the side instead of straight-on gives the wrong value. From the guided notes graduated cylinder example:

  • Looking down → reads 19.82 mL (too high)
  • Looking up → reads 19.62 mL (too low)
  • Looking straight at the meniscus → 19.70 mL (correct)

The slide's rule: read from higher, measurement appears lower; read from lower, measurement appears higher.

§ 06

Significant Figures 2.8

Counting sig figs (slide 60)

  1. All integers (1–9) are significant — they're read by the instrument.
  2. Captive zeros (between non-zeros, like 102) → always significant.
  3. Leading zeros (0.102) → not significant; just place holders.
  4. Trailing zeros → sometimes significant — only if the number has a decimal point.
    • 123.0 → 4 sig figs
    • 120 → 2 sig figs
    • 120. → 3 sig figs (the decimal makes the zero count)
Why this works (slide 60) The last significant digit is an estimate — therefore anything following it is just a placeholder.

Dimensional Analysis 2.6

The factor-label method. Set up so units cancel.

The steps (slide 54)

  1. Determine the given value and unit, and the unit you're seeking.
  2. Find conversion factors that relate the start and end units, directly or in a chain.
  3. Write the given quantity, then multiply by conversion factors. Each factor must have the previous unit in the bottom so it cancels.
1 day × 24 hr1 day × 60 min1 hr × 60 s1 min = 86,400 s

Common conversion factors (slide 55)

MassLengthVolume
1 lb = 454 g1 in = 2.54 cm1 gal = 3.785 L
1 kg = 1000 g1 mi = 1.61 km1 L = 1000 mL
1 lb = 16 oz1 mi = 5,280 ft1 mL = 1 cm³
1 g = 1000 mg1 ft = 12 in1 oz = 29.6 mL

Slide 56 · Hollie the dog

42.5 kg × (1000 g / 1 kg) × (1 lb / 454 g) = 93.6 lb
Slide 57 · Cow's daily milk

Bonus · 25 km/hr to mi/hr
25 km/hr × (1000 m / 1 km) × (100 cm / 1 m) × (1 in / 2.54 cm) × (1 ft / 12 in) × (1 mi / 5280 ft) = 15.5 mi/hr
§ 09

Density 2.3

D = mV

Rearranged: m = D × V and V = m / D.

Units

  • D → g/mL (liquids) or g/cm³ (solids)
  • m → g
  • V → mL or cm³ (they're equal!)
Key idea — slide 10 Liquids of different densities will layer themselves: the more dense substance sinks; the less dense one floats. A heavier (denser) ball sinks; a lighter (less dense) ball floats.

Slide 8 worked problems

Baseball
Determine the mass of an official MLB baseball with V = 210 cm³, D = 0.71 g/cm³.
m = D × V = 0.71 × 210 = 149 g
Golf ball
If a golf ball has density 1.13 g/mL and mass 45.9 g, what's its volume?
V = m / D = 45.9 / 1.13 = 40.6 mL
Copper block (water displacement)
Water rises from 20.0 mL to 25.9 mL → V = 5.9 mL. With D = 8.96 g/mL:
m = 8.96 × 5.9 = 52.9 g
Density from a graph — slide 9 If you graph mass (y-axis) vs. volume (x-axis), the data forms a straight line through the origin. This is a direct relationship, and the slope = density (with units of g/mL).
§ 10

Graphing 2.2

One variable is varied (the independent variable) and the other is measured (the dependent variable). Graphing illustrates the mathematical relationship.

Direct relationshipIndirect (inverse) relationship
As x ↑y ↑ (same direction)y ↓ (opposite direction)
ShapeStraight lineCurve approaching but never crossing axes
Equationy = kx (through origin) or y = kx + bxy = k
What k meansSlope of the lineArea under the curve at that point

Slope (slide 48 worked example)

Slope = Δy / Δx = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)

From slide 48 data points (2.7, 3.8) and (5.0, 7.5)
Slope = (7.5 − 3.8) / (5.0 − 2.7) = 3.7 / 2.3 ≈ 1.6
Equation: y = 1.6x (passes through origin) — a direct relationship.
Slide 51 key idea Lab data never forms a perfectly straight or perfectly curved line because of uncertainty in measurements. Draw a best-fit line, never connect-the-dots.
Spotting the relationship from an equation Both variables on the same side in the same location (both in the numerator) → direct.
One variable in the numerator and one in the denominator → indirect.
§ 11

Averaging, Weighted Avg & Sample Size

Not in the slide deck — built from standard chemistry conventions. Expect basic conceptual questions.

Simple Average (Mean)

x̄ = sum of all valuesnumber of values
Example
Trial masses: 5.21 g, 5.19 g, 5.23 g, 5.22 g
(5.21 + 5.19 + 5.23 + 5.22) / 4 = 20.85 / 4 = 5.21 g

Apply sig fig rules to your average — don't report more decimals than your data justifies.

Weighted Average

Use when some data points "count more" than others (different sample sizes, different abundances).

x̄weighted = Σ (value × weight)Σ weights
Classic chemistry example — atomic mass of chlorine
• Cl-35 at 75.77 % abundance, mass ≈ 34.97 amu
• Cl-37 at 24.23 % abundance, mass ≈ 36.97 amu

Atomic mass = (34.97 × 0.7577) + (36.97 × 0.2423) = 26.50 + 8.957 ≈ 35.45 amu

That's why chlorine's atomic mass on the periodic table is ~35.45, not a whole number.
Lab example
Group A: 5 trials averaging 4.20 g/mL. Group B: 2 trials averaging 4.50 g/mL.
Weighted by trial count: (4.20 × 5 + 4.50 × 2) / (5 + 2) = 30.0 / 7 = 4.29 g/mL
A simple mean of (4.20, 4.50) would give 4.35 — wrong, because Group A's data is based on more trials.

Sample Size

  • Larger sample size = more reliable results. Random errors cancel out.
  • One trial is not enough. A single measurement could be a fluke.
  • More trials → better estimate of both the true value AND the precision.
  • Outliers: in small samples, one weird point distorts the average. In big samples, outliers matter less.
  • Rule of thumb in chem lab: at least 3 trials before averaging.

Parts of a Science Report

A likely test target — these aren't in the slide deck either.

SectionWhat goes here
TitleWhat was the experiment? Specific, concise.
Purpose / ObjectiveOne sentence: what you set out to find or test.
HypothesisYour prediction, stated before doing the experiment ("If… then… because…").
MaterialsBulleted list of equipment and chemicals used.

§ 14

Self-Check Practice

Try these without looking. Answers below.

  1. Classify: "The solution turned blue." Observation or interpretation?
  2. Classify: "The copper reacted with the acid." Observation or interpretation?
  3. A target has 4 darts grouped tightly together but far from the bullseye. Precise? Accurate?
  4. Slide 4 equipment: name A, B, C, and D.
  5. How many sig figs: 0.00450, 4500, 4500.0, 4.50 × 10³?
  6. 12.11 g + 0.4 g + 1.115 g = ? (correct sig figs)
  7. 3.20 cm × 1.5 cm = ? (correct sig figs)
  8. A student measures aluminum density as 2.55 g/mL. Accepted value is 2.70 g/mL. % error (with sign)?
  9. Convert 4.5 km to mm using dimensional analysis.
  10. A block has mass 54.0 g and volume 20.0 mL. What is its density?
  11. Trials yield densities 1.02, 1.04, 1.03, 1.01 g/mL. What's the average?
  12. Two groups: Group 1 → 8 trials averaging 25.0°C; Group 2 → 2 trials averaging 26.0°C. What's the weighted average?
  13. Convert 272 cm to feet (slide 55).
  14. What is the slope of a line passing through (2.7, 3.8) and (5.0, 7.5)?
  15. On a graduated cylinder where the smallest line = 0.1 mL, you read between 6.5 and 6.6, about 9/10 of the way up. What's the measurement?

Answers

  1. Observation — you see the color.
  2. Interpretation — "reacted" explains what's happening.
  3. Precise, not accurate. Classic systematic-error pattern.
  4. A = graduated cylinder, B = beaker, C = ring stand, D = Bunsen burner.
  5. 0.00450 → 3; 4500 → 2; 4500.0 → 5; 4.50 × 10³ → 3.
  6. 12.11 + 0.4 + 1.115 = 13.625 → 13.6 g (0.4 has 1 decimal).
  7. 3.20 × 1.5 = 4.80 → 4.8 cm² (1.5 has 2 sig figs).
  8. (2.70 − 2.55) / 2.70 × 100 = +5.56 % (positive because experimental was less than theoretical).
  9. 4.5 km × (1000 m / 1 km) × (1000 mm / 1 m) = 4,500,000 mm or 4.5 × 10⁶ mm.
  10. D = 54.0 / 20.0 = 2.70 g/mL.
  11. (1.02 + 1.04 + 1.03 + 1.01) / 4 = 4.10/4 = 1.03 g/mL.
  12. (25.0 × 8 + 26.0 × 2) / 10 = 252/10 = 25.2 °C.
  13. 272 cm × (1 in / 2.54 cm) × (1 ft / 12 in) = 8.92 ft.
  14. (7.5 − 3.8) / (5.0 − 2.7) = 3.7 / 2.3 = 1.6.
  15. Between 6.5 and 6.6, estimate 9/10 → 6.59 mL (matches slide 39).
§ 15

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting that the decimal point matters for trailing zeros (120 vs 120.).
  • Writing more decimal places than your instrument allows.
  • Mixing up accuracy and precision — repeat: accuracy = bullseye, precision = grouping.
  • Getting the % error sign backwards. (+) means experimental was LESS; (−) means GREATER.
  • Citing "human error" as a source of error. The slides explicitly say don't do this.
  • Forgetting to multiply by 100 in % error.
  • Reading a graduated cylinder from the top of the meniscus instead of the bottom (for water).
  • Reading from an angle → parallax error.
  • Connecting graph data points dot-to-dot instead of drawing a best-fit line.
  • Forgetting to convert units before plugging into a formula (always check units cancel).
  • Treating beaker volume markings as precise — they're not.

Walk in confident, work neatly, and double-check sig figs at the end of every problem.
