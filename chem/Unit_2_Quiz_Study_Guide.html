Key Terms Glossary

Every "Terms you NEED to know" from your guided notes, plus the supporting vocabulary your teacher uses in problems. Grouped by topic for easy review.

Thinking Skills Topic 2.1

scientific method
A systematic approach scientists use to answer questions or solve problems: observe → hypothesize → test → confirm/reject → incorporate.
observation
A fact obtained using your senses or an instrument. No explanation attached.
interpretation (inference)
An attempt to explain the facts. Adds reasoning, cause, or judgment to an observation.
hypothesis
A testable prediction made before doing an experiment. Often in "If… then… because…" form.
theory
An explanation supported by repeated experiments providing the same data. Remains in place until data is found to disprove it.
independent variable
The variable you change on purpose to test its effect. Goes on the x-axis.
dependent variable
The variable you measure — its value depends on the independent variable. Goes on the y-axis.
control (controlled variable)
Any factor held constant during the experiment so it doesn't affect the result.

Graphing Topic 2.2

direct relationship
As one variable increases, the other increases. Forms a straight line. Equation: y = kx (through origin) or y = kx + b. The constant k is the slope.
indirect (inverse) relationship
As one variable increases, the other decreases. Forms a curve that approaches but never crosses the axes. Equation: xy = k.
slope
The steepness of a line: Δy/Δx = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). For a mass vs. volume graph, the slope is the density.
best-fit line
A straight line drawn through scattered data points to show the overall trend. Never connect lab points dot-to-dot.

Density Topic 2.3

density
The amount of mass compared to its volume. D = m/V. Units: g/mL (liquids) or g/cm³ (solids).
mass
The amount of matter in an object. Measured in grams (g) on a balance.
volume
The amount of space an object occupies. Measured in mL or cm³ (1 mL = 1 cm³).
water displacement
A method to find the volume of an irregular solid by submerging it in water and measuring how much the water level rises.

Metric System Topic 2.4

metric system
The international system of measurement based on powers of 10. Units relate by multiplying or dividing by 10, 100, 1000, etc.
base unit
The "starting" unit for a quantity, with no prefix attached. Gram (mass), liter (volume), meter (length), second (time).
prefix
A modifier (kilo, centi, milli, micro, nano, pico) attached to a base unit to express larger or smaller amounts.
kilo- (k)
10³ = 1000 times the base unit. 1 kg = 1000 g.
centi- (c)
10⁻² = one hundredth of the base unit. 1 m = 100 cm.
milli- (m)
10⁻³ = one thousandth of the base unit. 1 L = 1000 mL; 1 g = 1000 mg.
micro- (μ or u)
10⁻⁶ = one millionth of the base unit. Used for very small things.
nano- (n)
10⁻⁹ = one billionth of the base unit. Used for atoms and electrons.

Making Measurements Topic 2.5

graduated
Marked with a series of lines (graduations) to allow measurement. A graduated cylinder, ruler, or thermometer.
meniscus
The curved surface of a liquid in a narrow container. Water forms a concave meniscus (read the bottom); mercury forms a convex one (read the top).
parallax error
The reading mistake caused by viewing a measurement from an angle instead of at eye level. Fix: look straight on.
certain digits
The digits in a measurement that you know for sure based on the instrument's marked lines.
estimated digit
The last digit in a measurement — your best guess of the value between the smallest marked lines. Always one estimated digit, no more.

Dimensional Analysis Topic 2.6

dimensional analysis
A method for converting between units using conversion factors, set up so that unwanted units cancel out. Also called the factor-label method.
conversion factor
A ratio of two equivalent quantities in different units, used to convert between them. Example: (1 m / 100 cm) or (60 s / 1 min).
imperial system
The non-metric system of measurement used mostly in the U.S. Inches, feet, miles, pounds, ounces, gallons.

Accuracy & Error Topic 2.7

accuracy
How close a measurement is to the true / accepted value. The dartboard bullseye.
theoretical value
The accepted, true, or "book" value of a quantity. What the answer should be.
experimental value
The value you obtained in lab through measurement and calculation.
percent error
A measure of how far off your experimental value is from the theoretical value, expressed as a percentage. |theoretical − experimental| / theoretical × 100.
systematic error
An error that affects every measurement the same way (e.g., a balance that always reads 0.5 g too high). Causes high precision with low accuracy.
random error
Small unpredictable variations in measurements. Reduced by averaging more trials.
percent recovered (yield)
How much of an expected product you actually obtained. actual / expected × 100.

Precision & Significant Figures Topic 2.8

precision
(1) How close repeated measurements are to each other. (2) How finely an instrument can read — its smallest reliable division.
significant figures (sig figs)
All the digits in a measurement known for certain, plus the one estimated digit. They communicate how precise the measurement is.
captive zeros
Zeros between non-zero digits (e.g., 102). Always significant.
leading zeros
Zeros before the first non-zero digit (e.g., 0.0025). Never significant — they're placeholders.
trailing zeros
Zeros after the last non-zero digit (e.g., 120 or 1.20). Significant only if there's a decimal point.
uncertainty
The unavoidable "fuzziness" in any measurement — no instrument reads to an infinite number of decimal places.
rounding
Trimming a calculated answer to the correct number of sig figs. ≥ 5 rounds up; < 5 rounds down.

Data Analysis

average (mean)
The sum of all values divided by the number of values. Used to smooth out random errors across multiple trials.
weighted average
An average where each value is multiplied by its "weight" (how much it counts) before summing. Σ(value × weight) / Σ(weights). Used for atomic masses of isotopes.
sample size
The number of trials or data points collected. Larger samples give more reliable averages because random errors cancel out.
outlier
A data point that's noticeably different from the rest. Distorts averages more in small samples than in large ones.
trial
One complete run of an experiment or measurement. Best practice: at least 3 trials before averaging.

Lab Report

purpose / objective
The one-sentence statement of what the experiment is trying to find or test.
procedure
The numbered list of steps taken in the experiment — detailed enough that another scientist could reproduce it.
data
The raw measurements and observations collected during the experiment. Facts only, no interpretation.
analysis / calculations
Where you process the raw data — averages, density calculations, % error, unit conversions. Show all work with units and sig figs.
conclusion
The interpretation of your results. Did the data support the hypothesis? How close was the experimental value to the accepted value?
sources of error
Limitations of the equipment or procedure that could have affected accuracy. NOT "human error" — fix mistakes, don't list them.
§ 01

Thinking Skills 2.1

Observation vs. interpretation, and the scientific method.

Observation = a fact obtained directly using your senses (or an instrument reading). No explanation, no "why."
Interpretation (inference) = an attempt to explain the facts. It adds a reason, cause, or judgment.

Quick test: if the statement could be wrong because you're guessing why something happened, it's an interpretation.

StatementTypeWhy
The match burnsObservationYou see flame
The match reacts with oxygenInterpretationYou're explaining the cause
The metal gets warmerObservationYou feel/measure temperature
Heat transfers from the match to the metalInterpretationYou're explaining what's happening
Black soot is deposited on the metalObservationYou see it

The Scientific Method

  1. Observe something happening
  2. Think about why it's happening
  3. Form a testable hypothesis
  4. Test your hypothesis (experiment — change only one variable at a time)
  5. Confirm or reject the hypothesis
  6. Incorporate the new knowledge
Variables Vocab Independent variable = the one you change/vary. Dependent variable = the one you measure (it "depends" on the independent). Control = factors held constant so they don't affect the result. Theory = an explanation supported by repeated experiments; stays until disproved.
§ 02

Accuracy vs. Precision 2.7

These are not the same thing. Memorize the dartboard.

  • Accuracy — how close a measurement is to the true / accepted / theoretical value.
  • Precision — how close repeated measurements are to each other (reproducibility), AND how finely an instrument can read.
Dartboard patternPrecisionAccuracy
Darts scattered, none near bullseye✗✗
Darts tightly clustered, but far from bullseye✓✗
Darts spread out, but average near bullseye✗✓
Darts tightly clustered on the bullseye✓✓
Watch for this A common lab situation: high precision but low accuracy = systematic error. The instrument is consistent, but consistently wrong (e.g., a miscalibrated balance reads the same wrong value every time).
§ 03

% Error 2.7

% Error = theoretical − experimental theoretical × 100
  • theoretical value = accepted/true value (from a textbook or reference)
  • experimental value = what you measured in lab

Sign convention (without absolute value):

  • (+) error → experimental is lower than theoretical
  • (−) error → experimental is higher than theoretical

Worked examples

Example 1 · Calibration weight
A 100.00 g weight reads 101.41 g on the scale.
% Error = |100.00 − 101.41| / 100.00 × 100 = 1.41 %
Example 2 · Density of nickel
Experimental 7.59 g/mL, true 8.91 g/mL.
% Error = |8.91 − 7.59| / 8.91 × 100 = 14.8 %
Example 3 · Volume of a mole of gas
Bobby got 18.7 L, should be 22.4 L.
% Error = |22.4 − 18.7| / 22.4 × 100 = 16.5 %
Sources of error — what to cite 1. Limitations of the equipment (e.g., the balance only reads to 0.01 g).
2. NOT "human error" — your teacher's notes are explicit: if you made a mistake, you fix it; don't list it as a source of error.

Related: % Recovered (Yield)

% Recovered = actual recovered expected × 100

Example: recovered 12.56 g of 13.00 g expected → 12.56/13.00 × 100 = 96.6 % recovered, so % error = 3.4 %.

Lab Equipment & Measurements 2.5

How to read a graduated instrument

  1. Figure out what the smallest division represents.
  2. Read every digit that the lines give you for certain.
  3. Estimate ONE more digit between the smallest lines (mentally divide into 10 parts).
  4. Your measurement = all certain digits + 1 estimated digit.

Parallax Error

Looking at a measurement from the side instead of straight-on makes you read the wrong value. The graduated cylinder example from your notes:

  • Looking down → reads 19.82 mL (too high)
  • Looking up → reads 19.62 mL (too low)
  • Looking straight at the meniscus → reads 19.70 mL (correct)

Fix: read at eye level, straight on.

Ruler practice (Ruler Key 1 & 2)

  • Ruler 1: smallest division = 1's → read to the 0.1's place (1 decimal)
  • Ruler 2: smallest division = 0.1's → read to the 0.01's place (2 decimals)

The rule: a measurement is read to one decimal place beyond the smallest marked division.

§ 05

Significant Figures 2.8

Counting sig figs

  1. All non-zero digits (1–9) are significant.
  2. Captive zeros (between non-zeros, like 102) → always significant.
  3. Leading zeros (0.0025) → never significant; placeholders.
  4. Trailing zeros → significant only if there's a decimal point.
    • 123.0 → 4 sig figs
    • 120 → 2 sig figs
    • 120. → 3 sig figs (the decimal point makes the zero count)

C. Density 2.3

D = mV

Rearranged: m = D × V and V = m / D.

Units

  • D → g/mL (liquids) or g/cm³ (solids)
  • m → g
  • V → mL or cm³ (they're equal!)

Key behaviors

  • The denser substance sinks; the less dense floats.
  • A graph of mass vs. volume → straight line through origin → direct relationship; the slope = density.
Density problems

Baseball: V = 210 cm³, D = 0.71 g/cm³ → m = 0.71 × 210 = 149 g (2 sig figs: 150 g)

Golf ball: m = 45.9 g, D = 1.13 g/mL → V = 45.9 / 1.13 = 40.6 mL

Copper block (water displacement): Water rises from 20.0 mL to 25.9 mL, so V = 5.9 mL. With D = 8.96 g/mL:
m = 8.96 × 5.9 = 52.9 g

D. Graphing 2.2

Direct relationshipIndirect (inverse) relationship
As x ↑y ↑y ↓
Shapestraight linecurve approaching but never crossing axes
Equationy = kx (through origin) or y = kx + bxy = k
k meansslope of the linearea under the curve at that point

Slope formula: slope = Δy / Δx = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)

If you graph mass vs. volume for a substance and get a straight line through the origin, the slope is the density.

Important Real lab data never makes a perfectly straight or perfectly curved line — there's always uncertainty in measurements. Draw a best-fit line, not a connect-the-dots zigzag.
§ 07

Averaging, Weighted Averaging & Sample Size

Not in your guided notes — built from standard chemistry conventions. Expect conceptual questions.

Simple Average (Mean)

x̄ = sum of all valuesnumber of values
Example
Trial masses: 5.21 g, 5.19 g, 5.23 g, 5.22 g
(5.21 + 5.19 + 5.23 + 5.22) / 4 = 20.85 / 4 = 5.21 g

Apply sig fig rules to your average — don't report more decimals than your data justifies.

Weighted Average

Use when some data points "count more" than others (different sample sizes, different reliability, or different proportions).

x̄weighted = Σ (value × weight)Σ weights
Classic chemistry example — atomic mass
Chlorine has two isotopes:
• Cl-35 at 75.77 % abundance, mass ≈ 34.97 amu
• Cl-37 at 24.23 % abundance, mass ≈ 36.97 amu

Atomic mass = (34.97 × 0.7577) + (36.97 × 0.2423) = 26.50 + 8.957 ≈ 35.45 amu

That's why chlorine's atomic mass on the periodic table is ~35.45, not a whole number.
General lab example
Group A measured 5 trials averaging 4.20 g/mL; Group B measured 2 trials averaging 4.50 g/mL.
Weighted by trial count:
(4.20 × 5 + 4.50 × 2) / (5 + 2) = (21.0 + 9.0) / 7 = 4.29 g/mL

A simple mean of just (4.20, 4.50) would give 4.35 — but that's wrong because Group A's data is based on more trials.

Sample Size

  • Larger sample size = more reliable results. Random errors tend to cancel out.
  • One trial is not enough. A single measurement could be a fluke.
  • More trials → better estimate of the true value AND better estimate of precision.
  • Outliers: with a small sample, one weird data point distorts the average a lot. With a big sample, outliers matter less.
  • Rule of thumb in chem lab: at least 3 trials before averaging.

Parts of a Science Report

A likely test target since it isn't in the guided notes.

SectionWhat goes here
TitleWhat was the experiment? Specific, concise.
Purpose / ObjectiveOne sentence: what you set out to find or test.
HypothesisYour prediction, stated before doing the experiment ("If… then… because…").

§ 10

Self-Check Practice

Try these without looking at the notes. Answers below.

  1. Classify: "The solution turned blue." Observation or interpretation?
  2. Classify: "The copper reacted with the acid." Observation or interpretation?
  3. A target has 4 darts grouped tightly together but far from the bullseye. Precise? Accurate?
  4. How many sig figs: 0.00450, 4500, 4500.0, 4.50 × 10³?
  5. 12.11 g + 0.4 g + 1.115 g = ? (correct sig figs)
  6. 3.20 cm × 1.5 cm = ? (correct sig figs)
  7. A student measures the density of aluminum as 2.55 g/mL. The accepted value is 2.70 g/mL. What is the % error?
  8. Convert 4.5 km to mm using dimensional analysis.
  9. A block has mass 54.0 g and volume 20.0 mL. What is its density?
  10. Trials yielded densities of 1.02, 1.04, 1.03, 1.01 g/mL. What's the average?
  11. Looking at Ruler 1 (1's marked), the bar ends at the 6th line plus a little. To what precision should you record? Give an example.
  12. Two groups report: Group 1 → 8 trials averaging 25.0°C; Group 2 → 2 trials averaging 26.0°C. What's the weighted average temperature?

Answers

  1. Observation — you see the color.
  2. Interpretation — "reacted" explains what's happening at the molecular level.
  3. Precise, not accurate. Classic systematic error pattern.
  4. 0.00450 → 3; 4500 → 2; 4500.0 → 5; 4.50 × 10³ → 3.
  5. 12.11 + 0.4 + 1.115 = 13.625 → 13.6 g (0.4 has 1 decimal).
  6. 3.20 × 1.5 = 4.80 → 4.8 cm² (1.5 has 2 sig figs).
  7. |2.70 − 2.55| / 2.70 × 100 = 5.56 %.
  8. 4.5 km × (1000 m / 1 km) × (1000 mm / 1 m) = 4,500,000 mm or 4.5 × 10⁶ mm.
  9. D = 54.0 / 20.0 = 2.70 g/mL.
  10. (1.02 + 1.04 + 1.03 + 1.01) / 4 = 4.10/4 = 1.03 g/mL.
  11. Read to 0.1 cm — example "6.2 cm" or "6.4 cm" (one estimated digit past the smallest line).
  12. (25.0 × 8 + 26.0 × 2) / (8 + 2) = (200 + 52) / 10 = 25.2 °C.
§ 11

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting that the decimal point matters for trailing zeros (120 vs 120.).
  • Writing more decimal places than your instrument allows (a balance reading 5.0 g shouldn't get reported as 5.00 g).
  • Mixing up accuracy and precision — repeat after me: accuracy = bullseye, precision = grouping.
  • Citing "human error" as a source of error. Your teacher explicitly says don't do this.
  • Forgetting absolute value bars in % error (or forgetting to multiply by 100).
  • Reading a graduated cylinder from the top of the meniscus instead of the bottom (for water).
  • Reading from an angle → parallax error.
  • Connecting graph data points like a dot-to-dot puzzle instead of drawing a best-fit line.
  • Reporting m³ for a length × length calculation. Watch your units.

Walk in confident, work neatly, and double-check sig figs at the end of every problem.
