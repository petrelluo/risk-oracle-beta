Everything on the quiz lives in seven ideas, and they all hang off one picture: a heating curve. Energy goes in, particles speed up, temperature climbs — until a phase change, where the line goes flat and the energy quietly tears intermolecular forces apart instead. Master that story and the math (q = mcΔT plus the two phase-change terms) and you have the whole unit.

01 Heat, temperature & heat flow

Heat is the transfer of thermal (kinetic) energy from a hotter object to a colder one. It is energy in motion, not something an object "contains."

Temperature is the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample. A small cup of boiling water is hotter (higher temperature) than a warm bathtub, but the bathtub holds far more total energy because of its mass.

  • Direction of flow: heat always moves spontaneously from high temperature to low temperature (second law of thermodynamics), until both reach the same temperature — thermal equilibrium.
  • Units: the joule (J) is the SI unit. The calorie is older: 1 cal = 4.184 J. A food Calorie is a kilocalorie (1 Cal = 1000 cal).
  • System vs. surroundings: the system is the reaction or process you're studying; the surroundings are everything else (in a calorimeter, the water).
  • Key limit: you can never measure the total heat energy of a system — only the change, based on what it absorbs or releases.

Trap: "More heat" ≠ "higher temperature." The bathtub vs. cup question tests exactly this. Temperature = average KE; total thermal energy depends on mass too.

02 Endothermic & exothermic processes

Endothermic — energy IN

System absorbs heat from the surroundings. Surroundings get colder. ΔH is positive (+).

Melting, boiling, sublimation, photosynthesis, cold packs, ice melting in your hand.

Exothermic — energy OUT

System releases heat to the surroundings. Surroundings get warmer. ΔH is negative (−).

Freezing, condensation, combustion, neutralization, hand warmers, the sun radiating.

Mnemonic: exo = exit (out), endo = enter (in).

At the bond level: breaking bonds absorbs energy; forming bonds releases energy. If breaking costs more than forming gives back → endothermic. If forming gives back more → exothermic.

03 Heat capacity & specific heat

Specific heat capacity (c) is the amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K). Units: J/(g·°C) or J/(g·K).

  • Low c → fast temperature change. Metals (low c) heat up and cool down quickly. That's why a metal spoon in hot soup gets hot fast.
  • High c → resists temperature change. Water's c = 4.18 is unusually high, so water stores lots of energy with little temperature change — this moderates coastal climates.
  • °C vs. K: a change of 1 °C is the same size as a change of 1 K, so the numerical value of c is identical in either unit.

Quick test: given equal masses and equal heat, the substance with the lowest c shows the biggest temperature change.

04 Specific heat calculations — q = mcΔT

q = m · c · ΔT
q heat flow (J) m mass (g) c specific heat (J/g·°C) ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial (°C)

Rearrangements:   c = q/(mΔT)  •  m = q/(cΔT)  •  ΔT = q/(mc).

Sign of q (for the object): positive if it absorbs heat (warms up), negative if it releases heat (cools down).

Worked example (from the deck)

4.0 g of glass warms from 274 °C to 314 °C by absorbing 32.0 J. Find c.

ΔT = 314 − 274 = 40 °C → c = q/(mΔT) = 32.0 / (4.0 × 40) = 0.20 J/g·°C.

If that glass then cools from 325 K to 275 K: ΔT = 50 K = 50 °C, so q = (0.20)(4.0)(50) = 40 J released. Note Kelvin works fine for a temperature change.

05 Enthalpy (ΔH)

Enthalpy (H) is the heat content of a system at constant pressure. The enthalpy change (ΔH) is the heat absorbed or released by a reaction at constant pressure — that is, ΔH = qp.

  • ΔH negative (−) → exothermic (heat released; products lower energy than reactants).
  • ΔH positive (+) → endothermic (heat absorbed; products higher energy than reactants).
  • Enthalpy is a state function: ΔH depends only on the initial and final states, not the path (basis of Hess's Law).
  • Reverse a reaction → the sign of ΔH flips.

Example: a reaction with ΔH = −109 kJ releases 109 kJ and is exothermic.

06 Calorimetry

Calorimetry is measuring the heat absorbed or released by a reaction or process. It rests on conservation of energy: with no heat lost to the environment,

−qblock/reaction = qwater

"It's all about the water." Heat released by the system flows into the water (warming it); heat absorbed by the system pulls energy from the water (cooling it).

  • Water heats up → reaction is exothermic → ΔHrxn is negative.
  • Water cools down → reaction is endothermic → ΔHrxn is positive.

Coffee-cup calorimeter

Nested Styrofoam cups (insulation), a stirrer, a thermometer, water (surroundings), and the sample (system). Open to air → constant pressure → measures ΔH for solution reactions.

Bomb calorimeter

Sealed rigid steel chamber with an O₂ inlet, ignition leads, a motorized stirrer, thermometer, and surrounding water. Constant volume → built for combustion reactions.

Worked example (from the deck)

A 59.0 g metal heated to 100.0 °C is dropped into 100.0 mL water (100.0 g) at 23.7 °C; final T = 27.8 °C. Find c of the metal.

Step 1 — water: qwater = (100.0)(4.18)(27.8 − 23.7) = 1713.8 J absorbed.

Step 2 — metal: −qmetal = qwater, ΔTmetal = 27.8 − 100.0 = −72.2 °C.
c = 1713.8 / (59.0 × 72.2) ≈ 0.40 J/g·°C.

07 Heating curves & multi-step phase-change problems

A heating curve plots temperature vs. heat added. It has five parts: three sloped regions (a single phase warming) and two flat plateaus (phase changes).

  • Sloped regions (warming solid / liquid / gas): particles move faster, average kinetic energy and temperature rise. Use q = mcΔT.
  • Flat plateaus (melting, boiling): temperature is constant; energy goes into breaking intermolecular forces (potential energy rises, KE constant). Use q = mΔH.
  • The melting point = freezing point (same temperature); melting absorbs energy, freezing releases it. Same for boiling = condensation.
  • The boiling plateau is longer than the melting plateau because ΔHvap (2260 J/g) ≫ ΔHfus (334 J/g) — it takes far more energy to fully separate particles into a gas than to let them flow as a liquid.

The full multi-step equation (ice below 0 °C → steam above 100 °C):

Q = m·cs·ΔT + m·ΔHfus + m·cl·ΔT + m·ΔHvap + m·cg·ΔT
① warm solid② melt③ warm liquid④ boil⑤ warm gas

Worked example — 50.0 g ice (−20 °C) → steam (120 °C)

① warm ice50 × 2.10 × 202 100 J
② melt50 × 33416 700 J
③ warm water50 × 4.18 × 10020 900 J
④ boil50 × 2260113 000 J
⑤ warm steam50 × 1.89 × 201 890 J
Total154 590 J ≈ 154.6 kJ

Vaporization dominates — it's by far the largest term. A cooling curve is the exact reverse, and its phase changes (condensation, freezing) are exothermic.
